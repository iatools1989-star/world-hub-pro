### [0:48] Step 1 — The right format

Step one: pick the right format, because the wrong one buries everything good about you.

`[VISUAL: comparação lado a lado dos 3 formatos, com "Combination" destacado]`

There are three classic formats: chronological, functional, and combination. Chronological lists jobs in reverse order — useless when you don't have jobs. Functional focuses purely on skills, but recruiters don't love it because it feels like you're hiding something. Your answer is the **combination format**: skills near the top, then whatever experience you do have underneath. One page, clean columns, no graphics. That's it.

### [1:40] Step 2 — Header

Step two: the header. This looks simple, but I see mistakes here every single week.

`[VISUAL: exemplo de header anotado com checks verdes]`

Include your full name in the biggest font on the page, a professional email — firstname.lastname plus something at gmail, not partyperson2019 — your phone number, your city and state, and your LinkedIn URL and a portfolio link if you have one. Do not include your full street address, a photo, your age, or your marital status. In the U.S., that information can trigger discrimination concerns, and ATS software doesn't need it.

### [2:25] Step 3 — Professional summary

Step three: replace the outdated objective statement with a professional summary.

`[VISUAL: lado esquerdo "Objective (outdated)" riscado; lado direito "Professional Summary" em verde]`

Nobody wants to read "Seeking a challenging opportunity to leverage my skills." Instead, write two or three lines that answer three questions: who you are, what you can do, and what you want. Here's a template you can steal: "Detail-oriented recent graduate with coursework and hands-on projects in [field]. Skilled in [two hard skills], with strong communication and teamwork experience from [volunteer work, club, or part-time gig]. Seeking an entry-level [role] where I can contribute to [type of result]." That's specific, confident, and keyword-rich — and it took you three minutes.

### [3:35] Step 4 — Education

Step four: when you have no work history, education becomes your anchor section, so make it work harder.

`[VISUAL: exemplo de seção de educação com coursework, GPA e projetos]`

List your school, degree, and expected or actual graduation date. Include your GPA only if it's 3.5 or higher. Then add "Relevant Coursework" — four to six classes that match the job. If you're applying for a marketing role and you took Digital Marketing, Statistics, and Consumer Behavior, that's evidence. Add academic projects here too: capstones, research papers, group presentations. A project where you analyzed data in Excel or built a website for a local business is real, demonstrable experience. Treat it like a job, with bullets and results.

### [4:40] Step 5 — Experience sem emprego formal

Step five is where the whole resume changes. We're going to convert your "non-work" life into experience bullets.

`[VISUAL: ícones aparecendo um por um: volunteer, club, sports, freelance, personal project, side gig]`

Here's what counts: volunteer work, student clubs and organizations, sports teams, freelancing, personal projects, babysitting, tutoring, lawn care, helping run a family business — all of it. The key is how you write the bullet. Use this formula: action verb, plus what you did, plus a measurable result.

`[VISUAL: fórmula gigante na tela: "ACTION VERB + TASK + RESULT"]`

Weak bullet: "Helped with social media." Strong bullet: "Created and scheduled 12 weekly Instagram posts for a student club, growing followers from 200 to 900 in one semester." Same activity. Totally different impression. Write every bullet in past tense, start with strong verbs like organized, built, managed, designed, raised, or trained, and include a number whenever you possibly can. Numbers make claims believable.

### [6:18] Step 6 — Skills

Step six: the skills section, which is your secret weapon against the ATS.

`[VISUAL: print de uma descrição de vaga com palavras circuladas, depois a seção de skills correspondente]`

Open the job description, highlight every hard skill and tool it mentions — Excel, Salesforce, Canva, Python, Google Analytics, whatever it is. If you genuinely have that skill, put it on your resume using the exact same word. The ATS is a matching system; it scans for the employer's keywords. Group skills into "Technical Skills" and "Soft Skills," keep soft skills limited to four or five, and never claim a tool you can't explain in an interview.

### [7:20] Step 7 — Formatação ATS

Step seven: formatting, because up to three quarters of resumes get rejected by software before a human ever sees them.

`[VISUAL: tela de ATS parseando um currículo — versão limpa passa, versão cheia de ícones falha]`

The rules are simple. Use standard section headings like "Experience" and "Education" — no cute names. Avoid tables, text boxes, columns with graphics, icons, and photos. Use standard fonts: Arial, Calibri, Garamond, or Helvetica, size 10 to 12. Save and send as a PDF unless the posting explicitly asks for a Word file. Name the file "First Name Last Name Resume 2026.pdf" — recruiters open hundreds of these every week, and a clean filename signals professionalism.

### [8:25] Erros para deletar

Before you hit send, delete these five mistakes.

`[VISUAL: contagem regressiva 5→1 com cada erro]`

One: typos — run it through a grammar tool and have one other human read it. Two: longer than one page — entry level means one page, period. Three: generic bullets copied from the internet — recruiters have read them a thousand times. Four: an unprofessional voicemail greeting or email address. And five: lying about skills or experience. It gets caught in the interview, or worse, after you're hired.
